\IEEEPARstart{C}{ombinatorial} optimization problems (COPs), such as the Traveling Salesman Problem (\TSP{}) and the Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (\CVRP{}), have broad applications in logistics, transportation, and network design~\cite{TSPAPP}. As NP-hard problems, they are typically tackled by heuristic solvers. Classical solvers such as LKH-3~\cite{lkhtsp,lkhcvrp} and HGS~\cite{hgs} achieve near-optimal performance through decades of manual algorithm engineering, while recent neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) methods~\cite{MLCO,RL4CO,neuopt,2optlearning} aim to learn heuristic rules automatically from data. Despite their promise, the practical adoption of neural routing solvers remains limited by a fundamental challenge: \emph{generalization}. A solver trained on one distribution should remain effective, efficient, and reliable when directly applied to instances with different scales, structures, and benchmark distributions. However, recent generalization-focused studies have shown that many neural routing solvers degrade sharply under such zero-shot evaluation, and some even fall behind simple construction heuristics such as nearest neighbor and random insertion~\cite{nrs_survey}. This suggests that strong in-distribution performance on synthetic instances does not necessarily indicate practical solving ability.

This limitation is particularly important because the conventional evaluation protocol in NCO often emphasizes scalability on synthetic uniform instances rather than true zero-shot generalization. In many cases, separate models are trained and tested on matched instance scales or distributions, making the reported results closer to multi-model in-distribution performance than single-model generalization. Recent evaluations therefore advocate a stricter protocol: train one model on a narrow source distribution, and test it directly on diverse benchmark instances without retraining, fine-tuning, or instance-specific tuning~\cite{nrs_survey}. Under this view, the central question is no longer whether a neural solver can perform well on instances similar to its training data, but whether the learned heuristic can transfer robustly to realistic and heterogeneous instances.

Learning-guided optimization (LGO)~\cite{learn2branch,learn2delegate,neurolkh} offers a promising path toward this goal by embedding neural signals into classical heuristic frameworks. Instead of replacing the entire solver with an end-to-end neural policy, LGO uses learning to improve specific algorithmic components while preserving the robustness, scalability, and convergence behavior of mature solvers~\cite{learn2delegate,deepaco,learn2seg,huang2024contrastive}. Among LGO methods, learning to guide Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)~\cite{ACO,maxmin} is a representative direction for neural-assisted population-based search~\cite{deepaco,gtgaco}. ACO constructs candidate solutions through probabilistic transitions biased by heuristic priors and dynamically updated pheromones. Its population-based nature and iterative pheromone mechanism make it naturally suitable for exploration, adaptation, and generalization across diverse instances.

However, existing neural ACO methods do not fully exploit these advantages. Most of them learn static edge-level guidance from the input instance: some methods initialize the heuristic prior~\cite{deepaco,gfacs}, while others learn both heuristic and pheromone-like matrices~\cite{gtgaco}. In all cases, the neural model produces guidance from instance geometry \emph{only once}, and the resulting heatmap is then fixed throughout the entire ACO search. This design creates two related problems. First, it induces a \emph{search-dynamics mismatch}: during training, the model sees only static instance features, while during inference it is deployed inside a dynamic search process where pheromones, incumbent solutions, and explored regions evolve over hundreds of iterations. Second, it weakens generalization: a static heatmap learned from a narrow source distribution may encode distribution-specific edge preferences that fail to remain useful when the search trajectory changes on out-of-distribution benchmark instances. Consequently, the neural signal may become less helpful exactly when adaptive guidance is most needed.

\noindent\textbf{Dynamic Neural ACO.}
To address this limitation, we first introduce \textbf{\DyNACO{}}, a search-dynamics-aligned base model for neural ACO\@. Instead of training a model to emit a single static heatmap, \DyNACO{} trains a policy over the \emph{full search trajectory}. The policy periodically observes the evolving pheromone distribution and incumbent solution, extracts state-aware representations of the current optimization status, and emits updated edge-level guidance for the next search phase. We formalize this process as a \emph{semi-Markov Decision Process}, where each high-level policy action influences a block of low-level ACO iterations. This formulation aligns neural guidance with the evolving search-state interface of ACO, enabling the learned signal to adapt to different search stages rather than committing to a fixed prediction at initialization. As a result, \DyNACO{} learns not only where good edges may exist, but also how guidance should change as the search progresses.

Although dynamic guidance resolves the search-dynamics mismatch of static neural ACO, a single dynamic policy may still converge to a narrow search behavior. This creates a \emph{search-behavior mismatch} for population-based optimization: robust ACO benefits from complementary exploration and exploitation behaviors, while one neural guidance field imposes one bias on all ants. This is problematic for zero-shot generalization, where different test instances may require different exploration--exploitation trade-offs. On some instances, the solver should intensify around reliable pheromone structures; on others, it should actively counteract early bias and escape over-reinforced regions. This observation connects neural ACO to the broader principle that diversity should be useful rather than merely syntactic: the search benefits only when different samples or policies expose complementary high-quality regions. Recent NCO methods have made the same point from the construction-solver side. POMO exploits problem symmetries by forcing different initial actions~\cite{pomo}; MDAM assigns different decoders to encourage distinct construction policies~\cite{mdam}; Poppy trains a population of policies by updating the best-performing member on each instance~\cite{poppy}; and PolyNet shows that a single decoder can learn multiple complementary solution strategies without hand-crafted first-move diversity~\cite{polynet}. These works establish diversity as a central ingredient of neural search, but they mainly diversify one-shot construction policies. Neural-guided ACO requires a different object of diversification: behavior-conditioned transition distributions should remain complementary throughout a long, pheromone-driven trajectory while still sharing the same incumbent solution and colony memory.

\noindent\textbf{Trajectory-Diverse Neural ACO.}
To further strengthen this base model, we then introduce \textbf{\DyNACS{}} (Dynamic Neural Ant Colony Search) on top of \DyNACO{}. Rather than treating neural guidance as a single global search bias, \DyNACS{} parameterizes dynamic guidance as a mixture of behavior-conditioned trajectory distributions. Each behavior is optimized according to the quality of the search trajectory it induces, so diversity is encouraged only when it improves the overall search population. During inference, online behavior routing distributes search effort according to recent performance on the current instance. In this way, the neural component induces heterogeneous ant behavior without running independent solvers: it preserves the state-aware adaptation of \DyNACO{}, while strengthening exploration, reducing premature convergence, and improving robustness under distribution shift.

\noindent\textbf{Scalable Search Substrate.}
Both \DyNACO{} and \DyNACS{} rely on a scalable search substrate. A method that generalizes only on small instances is insufficient for practical routing, while a scalable method that relies on retraining or distribution-specific tuning does not satisfy zero-shot deployment. To make dynamic and trajectory-diverse neural guidance practical at scale, we pair the policy with a perturbation-based ACO backend on a sparse $K$-nearest-neighbor graph and a \emph{scope-restricted refinement} (SRR) mechanism. Since each perturbation changes only a localized part of the current solution, SRR confines local search to the neighborhood of the policy-selected edges. This design brings three benefits. First, it stabilizes credit assignment because the post-refinement reward remains causally tied to the policy action. Second, it provides within-scope convergence by exhaustively applying local 2-opt moves around the perturbed edges until no further improvement can be found. Third, it reduces the per-ant refinement cost from $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(M\cdot K)$, making training and inference feasible even at the 100,000-node scale.

We evaluate \DyNACS{} under a generalization-focused protocol on both \TSP{} and \CVRP{}. Unlike the conventional in-distribution setting, we train only one model on 1K-node instances and test it directly on benchmark datasets with different scales and distributions. This setting is substantially more challenging because the model is not retrained, fine-tuned, or specialized for each benchmark subset. Under this zero-shot protocol, \DyNACS{} achieves an average gap of $1.98\%$ on \TSP{} and $2.80\%$ on \CVRP{}, outperforming all state-of-the-art neural baselines under matched evaluation. These results show that neural guidance can remain effective beyond synthetic training distributions when it is embedded into an adaptive population-based search process and trained to respond to search dynamics.

Further analyses reveal why the proposed method generalizes better than static neural ACO\@. The learned policy does not simply reinforce short or high-probability edges from the input geometry. Instead, it adapts to the evolving pheromone landscape, suppresses over-reinforced edges when the search begins to stagnate, and redirects ants toward under-explored yet promising regions. The trajectory-diverse extension further maintains complementary search behaviors across the ant population, allowing the solver to balance intensification and diversification according to the instance being solved. These findings suggest that generalization in neural routing solvers should not be pursued only through larger models or broader training datasets, but also through better alignment between learned guidance and the dynamics of the underlying heuristic search.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We identify zero-shot generalization as the central challenge for neural-guided ACO, and show that static heatmap-based guidance suffers from search-dynamics mismatch and poor adaptability to out-of-distribution search trajectories.
\item We propose \DyNACO{}, a search-dynamics-aligned neural guidance framework formulated as a semi-MDP, where a policy periodically observes pheromones and incumbent solutions to inject state-aware edge-level guidance throughout the ACO trajectory.
\item We introduce trajectory-diverse \DyNACS{} to address the search-behavior mismatch of single-guidance neural ACO\@. The method learns complementary search behaviors and uses online behavior routing to adapt search effort among them, improving useful diversity and robustness under distribution shift.
\item We design a scalable perturbation-based ACO backend with scope-restricted refinement, preserving stable credit assignment while enabling efficient local convergence on sparse graphs up to the 100,000-node scale.
\item We conduct a generalization-focused evaluation by training only on 1K-node instances and testing zero-shot on diverse benchmark datasets. \DyNACS{} achieves $1.98\%$ gap on \TSP{} and $2.80\%$ gap on \CVRP{}, outperforming all state-of-the-art neural baselines under the same protocol.
\end{itemize}
