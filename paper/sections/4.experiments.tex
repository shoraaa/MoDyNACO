
We evaluate \DyNACS{} with emphasis on \emph{zero-shot in-problem generalization}. Unless otherwise specified, a single model trained on uniformly distributed 1K-node synthetic instances is directly tested on benchmark \TSP{} and \CVRP{} instances with different scales, structures, and distributions. This setting differs from the conventional neural combinatorial optimization protocol, where models are often trained and tested on matched synthetic distributions, or where different models are trained for different scales. Our goal is to evaluate whether the learned guidance transfers beyond the training distribution while preserving the efficiency and reliability of the underlying ACO search.

The evaluation follows the progression of the method. We first compare the full \DyNACS{} framework with classical solvers, neural routing solvers, and neural-guided ACO baselines under the zero-shot benchmark protocol. We then isolate the contribution of trajectory-diverse guidance by comparing the shared backend, the single-prior dynamic model \DyNACO{}, and the full \DyNACS{} model. Next, we study the main algorithmic mechanisms through ablations, with particular attention to whether SRR preserves policy credit assignment while maintaining local-search quality. Finally, we analyze runtime, neural overhead, training efficiency, and learned guidance behavior to clarify how the method scales in practice.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Training setup.}
All experiments use a fixed random seed and deterministic single-run evaluation. Training instances are generated online, with coordinates sampled uniformly from $[0,1]^2$, yielding 320 training instances in total (32 per epoch $\times$ 10 epochs). We train with PPO on a single RTX 5090 GPU and Ryzen 9 7950X CPU with 16 cores. Default \DyNACS{} hyperparameters are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}, and baseline configurations are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:setup}.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Benchmark datasets.}
For the main zero-shot evaluation, we follow the proposed generalization-focused benchmark protocol. The \TSP{} test suite includes instances from TSPLIB~\cite{tsplib}, National TSP~\cite{nationaltsp}, VLSI TSP~\cite{vlsitsp}, and the 8th DIMACS Implementation Challenge for TSP~\cite{dimacs8tsp}. These datasets cover geographic, industrial, clustered, and benchmark-generated structures. The \CVRP{} test suite includes instances from CVRPLIB~\cite{cvrplib}, including Set X and large-scale AGS instances~\cite{ags}. We include only standard Euclidean instances with available best-known solutions and without additional side constraints such as fixed edges, fixed route numbers, or duration limits.

This benchmark construction is substantially more challenging than synthetic in-distribution testing. The training distribution contains only uniform 1K-node instances, whereas the test suite contains benchmark instances with diverse structures and scales. Therefore, strong performance on this suite indicates zero-shot transfer across both scale and distribution.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Candidate graph and backend.}
The candidate graph is a symmetric $K$-nearest-neighbor graph with $K{=}32$, with ties broken by node index. A backup list of 64 additional neighbors is maintained for fallback~\cite{ACOTSP-MF}. For \TSP{}, the backend follows the perturbation-based ACO design adapted from~\cite{faco}. For \CVRP{}, we design a capacity-aware perturbation-based backend with scope-restricted refinement, described in Appendix~\ref{app:cvrp}.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Baselines.}
We compare with four groups of methods. First, we include simple construction heuristics, including Nearest Neighbor and Random Insertion. These baselines are important because recent generalization-focused evaluations show that many neural solvers can degrade below simple heuristics under benchmark distribution shift. Second, we include strong classical solvers, including LKH-3~\cite{lkhtsp,lkhcvrp} for \TSP{} and HGS~\cite{hgs} and AILS-II~\cite{ails2} for \CVRP{}. Third, we compare with representative neural routing solvers, including BQ~\cite{bq}, LEHD~\cite{lehd}, SIL~\cite{sil}, ICAM~\cite{icam}, ELG~\cite{elg}, INViT~\cite{invit}, L2R~\cite{l2r}, DGL~\cite{dgl}, ReLD~\cite{reld}, L2C-Insert~\cite{l2c}, H-TSP~\cite{htsp}, DACT~\cite{dact}, NeuOpt~\cite{neuopt}, GenSCO~\cite{gensco}, DRHG~\cite{drhg}, and Fast T2T~\cite{fastt2t}. Finally, we compare with neural-guided ACO methods, including DeepACO~\cite{deepaco}, GFACS~\cite{gfacs}, GTG-ACO~\cite{gtgaco}, and HeatACO~\cite{heataco}.

We also include the unguided perturbation-based ACO backend as a direct ablation baseline. This isolates the contribution of neural guidance, since \DyNACO{}, \DyNACS{}, and the unguided backend share the same candidate graph, perturbation mechanism, refinement procedure, and search budget.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Metrics.}
We report three complementary metrics. \emph{Effectiveness} is measured by the average optimality gap to the best-known solution (BKS):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Gap} =
\frac{\mathrm{Obj.}-\mathrm{BKS}}{\mathrm{BKS}}
\times 100%.
\end{equation}
\emph{Efficiency} is measured by wall-clock inference time. \emph{Reliability} is measured by the number of successfully solved instances. A method is considered unsuccessful if it runs out of memory, times out, fails to produce a feasible solution, or suffers severe performance breakdown. Results are grouped into three scale ranges: $(0,1\mathrm{K})$, $[1\mathrm{K},10\mathrm{K})$, and $[10\mathrm{K},100\mathrm{K}]$, with aggregate results also reported.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{TSP.}
On \TSP{}, \DyNACS{} achieves the best aggregate gap among neural methods and remains reliable across all scale groups. This is particularly important on medium and large benchmark instances, where many end-to-end neural solvers either degrade sharply or fail to solve all instances. Compared with the unguided ACO backend, \DyNACS{} consistently improves solution quality, confirming that the neural policy contributes useful guidance beyond the handcrafted perturbation-based search. Compared with neural baselines, \DyNACS{} combines stronger effectiveness with full reliability, avoiding the severe degradation observed in several construction-based and heatmap-based methods.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{CVRP.}
On \CVRP{}, \DyNACS{} also achieves the best overall gap among neural methods and solves all instances. This result is notable because \CVRP{} introduces capacity constraints and route-feasibility decisions that are absent in \TSP{}. The capacity-aware backend handles feasibility, while the neural policy provides dynamic edge-level guidance conditioned on pheromone and incumbent states. The consistent improvement over the unguided backend indicates that \DyNACS{} learns transferable search signals rather than merely exploiting \TSP{}-specific structure.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Comparison with simple heuristics.}
The inclusion of Nearest Neighbor and Random Insertion tests whether neural methods provide value beyond simple handcrafted rules. \DyNACS{} substantially outperforms these heuristics on both problems while preserving full reliability. This is important because several neural baselines degrade below simple heuristics under benchmark distribution shift, suggesting that strong synthetic performance does not necessarily translate to zero-shot deployment.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Comparison with classical solvers.}
Classical solvers such as LKH-3, HGS, and AILS-II remain stronger in absolute solution quality, reflecting decades of specialized engineering. However, \DyNACS{} is not intended to replace these solvers directly. Instead, it shows that a compact neural policy can improve a scalable population-based optimizer and generalize across benchmark distributions with low overhead. This positions \DyNACS{} as a practical learning-guided optimizer rather than an end-to-end neural replacement for mature heuristics.

\subsection{Effect of Trajectory-Diverse Guidance}
\label{sec:trajectory-diverse-effect}

Table~\ref{tab:trajectory-diverse-effect} isolates the progression from the shared search substrate to the base model and then to the full model. The unguided backend tests the perturbation-based ACO substrate without neural guidance. \DyNACO{} adds Dynamic Neural ACO through one state-aware guidance field. \DyNACS{} builds on \DyNACO{} by adding behavior-conditioned trajectory guidance, allowing multiple learned behaviors to steer disjoint ant groups within the same search process. This comparison is the central test of the second method layer: whether trajectory-diverse guidance strengthens the single-behavior dynamic model, rather than merely benefiting from the same backend or a larger search budget. The consistent reduction from \DyNACO{} to \DyNACS{} shows that trajectory-diverse guidance improves the search process itself: heterogeneous behaviors, shared search memory, and online behavior routing together improve zero-shot benchmark generalization over the base dynamic policy.

Tables~\ref{tab:srr-training} and~\ref{tab:srr-inference} compare SRR with full local search (FLS) and truncated local search (TLS). TLS preserves more of the policy signal, but leaves solutions under-refined. SRR provides the best trade-off: it runs local search to convergence within the perturbation scope, preserving the causal link between the policy action and the post-refinement reward while maintaining low refinement cost.

\subsection{Training Efficiency}
\label{sec:training-efficiency}

\DyNACS{} converges in approximately 30 minutes for TSP-1K and roughly 4 hours for TSP-100K on our machine, using only 320 training instances. This is substantially lighter than many end-to-end neural solvers that require large offline datasets or long training schedules. The shared \DyNACO{} encoder keeps the parameter count essentially scale independent because the architecture operates on local $K$-NN neighborhoods; \DyNACS{} adds only lightweight behavior-conditioned guidance maps on top of this representation. \subsection{Learned Guidance Behavior}
\label{sec:guidance-behavior}

We now examine what dynamic neural guidance learns that static heatmaps cannot capture.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{The policy adapts its guidance to the current search phase.}
Standard neural-ACO methods produce a static heatmap that is identical whether the solver has run for 10 or 10,000 iterations. Because \DyNACO{} conditions on the evolving pheromone field and incumbent solution, its improvements grow with longer search horizons. This confirms that state-aware conditioning enables the policy to remain useful across different stages of the search.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{The policy learns to counteract ACO stagnation.}
Figure~\ref{fig:matrix_model} displays the pheromone matrix and corresponding neural guidance scores at successive iterations. As the search progresses, pheromone concentrations saturate and many edges converge toward $\tau_{\max}$, making the pheromone signal less informative. Despite this, the model outputs diverse guidance scores. In particular, over-reinforced edges often receive negative guidance, which redirects exploration away from stagnant regions. This selective counter-pressure is unavailable to static priors because they cannot observe the evolving pheromone landscape.

Table~\ref{tab:cross-scale} compares an in-scale model with the 1K-trained model on larger synthetic instances. The small degradation, and in some cases improvement, indicates that the learned policy is largely scale-invariant. This transferability stems from three design choices: the local $K$-NN architecture, normalized state-aware features, and a perturbation action space whose size does not grow with the number of nodes.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Guidance--pheromone dynamics are consistent across scales.}
Figure~\ref{fig:guidance_pheromone_dynamics} shows a consistent two-phase interaction between neural guidance and pheromone. Early in the search, the policy tends to cooperate with pheromone by amplifying promising signals. Later, as pheromone concentrates and risks premature convergence, the policy increasingly suppresses over-reinforced edges to preserve exploration. The volatility diagnostics further show that guidance changes sharply after the first pheromone feedback, then settles into smaller but persistent updates across later outer steps. This rapid adaptation followed by steady refinement is the behavior expected from search-dynamics-aligned guidance: the policy reacts strongly when the search state first becomes informative, then continues to recalibrate as the pheromone field evolves.
